ABSTRACT
Rapid, portable, and automated determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is critical for antimicrobial
susceptibility testing in decentralized settings. We present TCG-CMDA (Tree-Shaped Concentration Gradient generator enabled
by Capillary Microfluidic Design Automation), a machine learning–guided platform for the automated design of capillary
microfluidic chips that passively generate programmable concentration gradients. A key innovation of TCG-CMDA is its ability to
achieve fully synchronized, capillary-driven flow of two preloaded agents, enabling precise and reproducible gradient formation
without external pumps or valves. By integrating computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations, a neural network surrogate
model, and quasi-Newton optimization, the platform customizes tree-shaped geometries to match target mixing profiles. We
validate TCG-CMDA through dye flow visualization and MIC testing of Escherichia coli against gentamicin, showing high
concordance with conventional antibiotic susceptibility testing. The resulting chip supports a streamlined workflow consisting
of passive loading, autonomous gradient generation, and incubation (∼2 h), followed by direct endpoint assessment, making
it well suited for decentralized point-of-care applications. TCG-CMDA establishes a closed-loop, simulation-guided design
paradigm for capillary microfluidic systems. Although demonstrated here for MIC-oriented antimicrobial testing, the framework
is generalizable and can be extended to other gradient-dependent microfluidic applications through geometry reconfiguration and
fluid-specific optimization.

Introduction
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a major threat to global
health, with projections estimating 10 million annual deaths
by 2050 if left unaddressed [1]. The increasing prevalence of
multidrug-resistant pathogens across both community and hos-
pital settings has underscored the urgent need for accurate, rapid,
and decentralized tools to guide antimicrobial therapy. Rapid
determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is
critical for guiding antimicrobial therapy, monitoring resistance

trends, and enabling timely susceptibility assessment in both
centralized and resource-limited settings [2]. As a quantitative
measure of antimicrobial efficacy, MIC plays a central role
in tailoring patient-specific treatment regimens and informing
antimicrobial stewardship strategies.
While standard methods such as broth microdilution remain
the ISO 20776-1:2019-certified gold standard for MIC testing,
their reliance on 16–24-h incubation windows render them
suboptimal for time-sensitive or resource-limited clinical settings.
Semiquantitative techniques, including antibiotic gradient strips
(e.g., Etest), offer simplified protocols and faster readouts but are
constrained by operator-dependent variability, narrow dynamic
ranges, and less precise endpoint definitions. Automated systems
such as ASTar and Accelerate Phen have emerged as promising
alternatives, leveraging technologies like time-lapse microscopy,
morphokinetic modelling, and fluorescent in situ hybridization to
reduce assay times to 6–8 h [3]. However, these systems still rely
on centralized laboratory infrastructure and demand biosafety
compliance, restricting their use in decentralized or resource-
limited settings. While conventional MIC determination methods
remain foundational, recent technological advancements and
evolving diagnostic needs highlight the critical role of rapid,
automated, and point-of-care (POC) platforms in modernizing
antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) [4].
The growing emphasis on POC diagnostics, in the context of
AMR surveillance and personalized medicine, highlights the
importance of meeting ISO 22870:2006 criteria: reduced reagent
consumption, simplified workflows, minimal operator input, and
robust performance in resource-constrained environments [5, 6].
Microfluidic technologies are increasingly positioned to address
these needs, offering platforms that combine precision, portabil-
ity, and multiplexing capabilities [7]. These miniaturized systems
allow manipulation of microscale fluid volumes on chip-based
substrates, dramatically reducing assay duration, reagent usage,
and biohazard generation [8]. Among the various microfluidic
modalities, capillary-driven platforms are especially compelling
due to their ability to operate without external pumps, valves, or
actuators [9]. Instead, these systems rely on passive mechanisms
such as surface tension, wettability gradients, and geometry-
controlled capillary pressures, making them inherently suited for
field deployment and user-independent workflows [10].
Recent advances in capillary microfluidic chips (CMCs), along
with new capillaric elements and rapid prototyping methods,
have significantly expanded the capabilities of passive, on-chip
liquid handling. The capillaric field-effect transistor (cFET)
has emerged as a versatile flow-switching component [11], and
its reversible variant enables bidirectional, reusable switching
through autonomous circuit-level feedback [12]. At the archi-
tectural level, the microfluidic chain reaction (MCR) encodes
structurally programmed, conditional propagation of capillary
events, supporting multi-step assay sequencing without exter-
nal actuation [13]. High-resolution 3D printing now enables
monolithic, capillarity-driven diagnostic devices incorporating
stop/go flow control and timed fluid delivery [14], while overflow-
regulated metered aliquoting produces precise sample volumes
via self-filling reservoirs that automatically drain excess fluid
[15]. In parallel, self-coalescing flow phenomena generate pulse-
shaped reagent packets with minimal axial dispersion, facilitating
efficient on-chip reconstitution [16]. These capabilities integrate
with geometry-based mixers and flow-splitting networks that
distribute fluids into fixed-volume reaction chambers, enabling
highly reproducible reagent composition and parallelized end-
point processing.
Despite these advances, composition control on CMCs remains
constrained by laminar, diffusion-limited transport, which limits
natural mixing to primarily molecular diffusion [17–19]. This
poses substantial challenges in applications requiring precise
mixing and serial dilution, such as MIC testing, where gradient
generation across multiple reaction chambers must be both con-
sistent and reproducible. To address these limitations, researchers
have developed geometric strategies that enhance mixing by
leveraging controlled convection–diffusion interactions. These
include the integration of bends, constrictions, and junctions that
promote fluid folding and increase interfacial surface area [20–24]
as well as more sophisticated micromixer architectures designed
to induce chaotic advection or enhance diffusive transport
through complex flow patterns [25].
Among these strategies, tree-shaped microfluidic gradient gener-
ators (MGGs) have emerged as effective for creating predictable
and tunable concentration gradients [26, 27]. These devices utilize
a hierarchical branching topology to repeatedly split and recom-
bine fluid streams, enhancing interfacial contact and promoting
spatially stable gradient formation through controlled convective
partitioning and diffusive recombination. Tree-shaped MGGs
have found utility in a wide range of applications, from drug
screening [28], cell-based studies [29], droplet generation [30],
controlled reaction conditions [31], bacteria growth and chemo-
taxis [32] to combinatory antimicrobial susceptibility testing [33].
Their reproducibility, ease of fabrication, and compatibility with
capillary flow make them well suited for deployment in portable,
self-contained diagnostic devices [34].
Nonetheless, the design of tree-shaped gradient generators
remains a non-trivial task [26]. The concentration profile across
the output chambers is a function of channel widths, lengths,
branching symmetry, and mixing geometries, which interact non-
linearly under varying fluid properties and conditions. As a
result, optimizing such devices often requires multiple iterations
of computational modelling and empirical validation. Recent
work has proposed automated design strategies to address this
bottleneck, drawing analogies between fluidic networks and
electrical circuits [35], or using modular serial dilution elements
to construct arbitrary profiles [36].
More recently, hybrid methodologies that integrate computa-
tional fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations with machine learning
(ML) have shown significant promise in modelling microfluidic
behaviour and accelerating design workflows [37–39]. These
data-driven surrogate models allow rapid exploration of high-
dimensional design spaces [40], reducing reliance on time-
and resource-intensive full-physics simulations. A growing body
of literature now demonstrates the power of AI not only in
optimizing chip layouts and predicting fluid behaviour but also
in automating experimental protocols, identifying optimal opera-
tional regimes, and even discovering novel flow patterns or archi-
tectures. From droplet microfluidics to organ-on-chip systems,
AI-guided tools are revolutionizing how microfluidic devices are

In this work, we introduce TCG-CMDA: a Capillary Microfluidic

design and optimization of Tree-Shaped Concentration Gra-

targets and minimizes device footprint while preserving gradi-

utility, we designed and fabricated a TCG chip for MIC testing
of Escherichia coli against gentamicin, achieving full gradient
formation with only passive capillary actuation. This closed-
loop design pipeline integrates physical modeling, AI-driven
prediction, and microfabrication, establishing a generalizable
framework for programmable microfluidic systems. In this study,
we demonstrate its application to rapid AST/MIC testing, while
extension to other gradient-dependent assays represents a natural
and promising direction for future work.

Materials and Methods
The details on materials, chip fabrication protocols, computa-
tional modeling, and machine learning workflows are provided
in the (S1).
2.1
TCG Chip
The TCG chip was fabricated as a three-layer, capillary-driven
microfluidic device using a standardized laser micromachin-
ing and lamination process. Chip layouts were designed in
AutoCAD 2024 and transferred to a Trotec Speedy 360 FLEXX
CO2 system for precision cutting, with optimized kerf and
offset parameters to ensure geometric fidelity. Each layer was
inspected microscopically prior to alignment and laminated
under uniform pressure using laser-engraved fiducial mark-
ers to achieve airtight sealing. The assembled chip incorpo-
rated agent chambers, mixers, and reaction chambers into
a single monolithic architecture. Preliminary flow tests with
dyed solutions confirmed reproducible capillary actuation, syn-
chronized front propagation, and stable gradient formation
across devices. Complete fabrication details are provided in
S1.
2.2
MIC Test Protocols
MIC assays were performed on TCG chips to evaluate automated
serial dilution and passive mixing performance. Escherichia coli
(ATCC 11303) cultures were prepared on LB agar and incubated at
37◦C for 24 h before inoculation into LB broth at 106–108 CFU/mL.
Inoculated and non-inoculated broths were introduced through
separate chip inlets to generate serial dilution gradients, followed
by incubation at 37◦C for 2 h. Each reaction chamber (RC) was
pre-coated with 1 wt% TTC to indicate metabolic activity through
colorimetric change, and flow split from selected RCs were plated
for CFU enumeration.
A dual-agent susceptibility screening assay was performed using
E. coli–inoculated LB medium and gentamicin (10 µg/mL),
introduced through opposing inlets to generate binary dilution
gradients on-chip. As the two inputs mix within the microflu-
idic network, each reaction chamber corresponds to a defined
bacteria–gentamicin composition, rather than a conventional
fixed-inoculum, serial antibiotic dilution condition. The RFP-
expressing E. coli strain enabled visualization under a Nikon
Eclipse TE2000 fluorescence microscope, complemented by TTC-
based assays for colorimetric validation. Results were compared
with standard 96-well MIC assays performed under identical con-
ditions, confirming comparable outcomes with reduced sample
volume and manual handling. Data are reported as mean ± SD,
with analyses performed in GraphPad Prism v9.4.0 using one-
way ANOVA (Tukey’s post-hoc) and unpaired t-tests (p < 0.05).
Complete experimental details are provided in S1.
2.3
CFD Simulation
Computational modeling was conducted in COMSOL Multi-
physics (v6.1) to optimize the TCG chip geometry and char-
acterize capillary-driven flow behaviour. The Laminar Flow,
Phase Field, and Transport of Diluted Species modules were
coupled to capture the steady-state fluid dynamics, interfacial
evolution, and solute transport within the microchannel network.
Boundary conditions assumed incompressible flow with no-slip
walls and atmospheric inlet/outlet pressures. Experimentally
measured contact angles for PMMA, PSA, and TPE (Table S1)
were incorporated to accurately represent surface-dependent
capillary effects. Simulations used the physical properties of
water (ρ = 997 kg m−3, µ = 0.89 mPa⋅s, D ≈10−9 m2 s−1) as
a simplified transport model, with locally refined meshing at
junctions and constrictions to capture steep velocity and concen-
tration gradients. Although culture-medium-specific transport
coefficients were not explicitly parameterized, the functional
performance of the device under LB-based assay conditions was
experimentally validated through bacterial–gentamicin testing
and reaction-chamber crosstalk analysis over a 2 h incubation
period.
The analysis yielded detailed velocity, pressure, and concentra-
tion distributions, revealing symmetric capillary flow and stable
gradient formation across the reaction chambers. Simulated
profiles were in strong agreement with experimental dye-flow
and bacterial-mixing data, validating the design’s reliability for
pump-free, automated gradient generation and MIC testing.
Comprehensive simulation parameters and boundary conditions
are provided in S1 and S4.

2.5
Machine Learning Model Training
A comprehensive dataset comprising fluid dynamics and concen-
tration gradient profiles from multiple TCG chip configurations
was assembled to guide design optimization. Each configuration
reflected a unique combination of MC widths (ranging from
300 to 900 µm) and pitch numbers (0–9) across three rows in
the generalized TCG chip architecture. This dataset was used
to train a feedforward NN with four hidden layer designed to
predict the concentration distributions within RCs based on
structural parameters. The input layer incorporated four neurons
representing MC width and the pitch number for each row, while
two hidden layers were optimized to balance model accuracy and
computational efficiency. The output layer yielded normalized
concentration values within the RCs. The network was trained
on 80% of the dataset using the Adam optimizer and backprop-
agation, with the remaining 20% reserved for validation. The
trained NN model enabled rapid and accurate prediction of fluidic
behaviour across TCG designs, substantially reducing reliance
on computationally intensive CFD simulations during iterative
optimization, and thereby accelerating the chip development
pipeline.
2.6
Optimization Algorithm
To achieve target concentration profiles within the RCs while
preserving design simplicity and manufacturability, a gradient-
based optimization strategy was implemented. The optimization
objective was to fine-tune TCG chip configuration parameters to
yield desired concentration outputs with minimal geometric com-
plexity. The optimization process employed the limited-memory
Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno algorithm with bound con-
straints (L-BFGS-B), implemented via the SciPy library. This
method is well-suited for high-dimensional, bound-constrained
problems and was critical for maintaining design parameters
within physically realizable and fabrication-compatible lim-
its. By integrating the trained neural network as a surrogate
model within this framework, the optimization enabled rapid
and scalable exploration of the design space, reducing com-
putational overhead while ensuring experimentally achievable
configurations.

Results and Discussion
This study presents the development and validation of CMDA
platform to support the rational design and customization of
TCG chips for programmable, on-chip MIC testing, with an
emphasis on POC diagnostics (Scheme 1A). The TCG-CMDA
platform leverages simulation-informed design rules to generate
microfluidic layouts capable of forming predefined antibiotic
gradients suited for a variety of biological and clinical targets.
To achieve this, the platform systematically tunes geometrical
parameters of the chip, the pitch and width of mixing com-
ponents (MCs), to optimize convective partitioning, diffusive
mixing, and uniform fluid distribution across all output channels
(Scheme 1B).
The TCG chip incorporates two inlets for preloading distinct
agents: Agent 1, comprising microbial suspensions (e.g., E.coli or
other biological samples), and Agent 2, representing therapeutic
compounds such as antibiotics. In this study, both agents were
aqueous LB-based suspensions with low viscosity and similar
physicochemical properties, ensuring compatibility with spon-
taneous capillary flow within the laminated PMMA/PSA/TPE
architecture. Systematic evaluation across a broader range of
viscosities and surface tensions was beyond the scope of this
work and is reserved for future investigation. Upon introduction
of a buffer droplet, capillary forces initiate a tightly coordi-
nated flow sequence that begins with synchronized uptake of
the preloaded reagents, followed by controlled flow splitting
and channel-specific mixing throughout the gradient-generating
network. The chip incorporates specialized buffer-sensitive inlet
chambers designed to respond to minimal user input, triggering
autonomous actuation of all downstream microfluidic operations
without external hardware or controllers.
This entirely passive mechanism ensures robust gradient for-
mation across a wide range of environmental and operational
conditions, meeting key POC criteria such as operational sim-
plicity, disposability, and minimal infrastructure requirements.
The system’s preprogrammed gradient profile enables targeted
assays, such as MIC testing, to be performed using a single
buffer-initiated actuation step, making it especially valuable
for settings where time, equipment, and expertise are limited.
Following gradient formation and incubation under specified
conditions, the biological outcome becomes detectable within the
terminal RCs, either visually or through downstream optical or
electrochemical sensing strategies, as illustrated in Scheme 1C.
To demonstrate the applicability of the TCG-CMDA platform,
we designed a chip configuration for MIC assessment using
Escherichia coli as the model organism (Agent 1) and gen-
tamicin as the antibiotic compound (Agent 2). As shown in
Scheme 1D, the resulting concentration gradient across the
six RCs displayed a clear, dose-dependent microbial inhibition
pattern, consistent with conventional MIC testing standards. This
validation confirms that the TCG-CMDA platform enables pre-
cise control over fluid partitioning and gradient formation while
supporting reproducible and scalable fabrication. More broadly,

TCG chip framework (Figure 1E). In this design, MC-specific
parameters (W, P) were varied systematically while all other
structural features remained constant. This modular configu-
ration facilitates rapid customization and evaluation of chip
performance across diverse use cases, supporting robust and
reproducible gradient formation through passive microfluidic
control.
3.2
Agent Inlets and Chambers
CMC systems have faced challenges when dealing with multiple
sample inlets. They have to control the sample using nonau-
tomated method such as soft valves [42], or automated ones
such as passive valves [43] or entrapped air [44]. However,
these methods are generally incompatible with the three-layer
chip architecture employed in this study, which is constructed
from pressure-sensitive adhesive layers and laminated substrates
without embedded valve structures.
In early TCG chip designs featuring dual inlets (Figure S1A),
simultaneous manual loading of two fluids was required to

initiate mixing, resulting in unreliable device performance. This
limitation often necessitated the use of multichannel pipettes
to achieve synchronized loading, an impractical requirement in
POC contexts where minimal operator involvement is essential.
A subsequent version (Figure S1B) employed dried, preloaded
agents embedded within the MCs, but this design introduced
challenges in terms of rehydration uniformity, storage stability,
and transport logistics.
To overcome these limitations, we developed a novel Agent
Chamber architecture that enables reliable, passive sample han-
dling without the need for precise user coordination. Each
chamber interfaces with an independent agent inlet and is
geometrically configured to interact with both atmospheric air
at one edge and the central merging point via the MCs at
the opposite side (Figure 1C). During operation, each Agent
Chamber is loaded with a fixed volume (5 µL) of liquid sample,
which passively fills the chamber and stops at the air–fluid
interface. When buffer is subsequently introduced into the Buffer
Inlet (45 µL), capillary forces displace the fluid in each Agent
Chamber simultaneously toward the merging region, trigger-
ing autonomous synchronization and gradient formation. This
architecture eliminates the need for external control hardware
or preloading procedures, thereby enabling true one-step sample
introduction and actuation. The approach ensures reliable cap-
illary initiation and uniform flow dynamics, making it ideally
suited for deployment in low-resource or decentralized diagnostic
settings where robustness, simplicity, and reproducibility are
critical.
3.3
MCs Configurations
Once flow was initiated by buffer loading, the preloaded agents
within the Agent Chambers were passively drawn through a
series of interconnected MCs arranged in a hierarchical tree-
like configuration between upstream and downstream Mani-
folds. This branching architecture enables repeated splitting and
recombination of fluid streams across three cascading rows,
increasing interfacial surface area and driving convective folding
and diffusive mixing throughout the channel network. The
geometry of the MCs was systematically optimized to support
stable flow propagation, customizable mixing behaviour, and
reproducible concentration gradient generation (Figure 1D).
Two key geometric parameters were varied: channel width
(W), ranging from 300 to 900 µm, and number of pitches
(P), ranging from 0 to 9. These variables were chosen due
to their direct influence on flow resistance, fluid front syn-
chronization, and mixing efficacy. All other design parameters,
such as channel height, surface energy, and substrate material
properties, were held constant to isolate the effects of MC
geometry.
Building on this generalized TCG chip design, four distinct
configurations were fabricated to investigate how MC geometry
modulates capillary flow dynamics and mixing performance
(Figure 1E,a–d). The first three configurations (a, b, c) focused on
varying the channel width while maintaining constant channel
length and eliminating pitch features, thereby isolating the effect
of MC width on flow distribution. The fourth configuration
(d) introduced an extended channel length and incorporated
pitch-based curvature by adding one full pitch cycle, which intro-
duced alternating expansion–constriction zones and additional
bending segments to enhance mixing. The following sections
provide a comparative analysis of these configurations using
two-phase capillary flow simulations and experimental mixing
quantification, enabling a deeper understanding of how passive
geometric elements can be tuned to regulate gradient fidelity in
CMC.
3.4
Reaction Chambers
At the final stage of gradient formation, the six RCs collect
the mixed outputs, serving as the primary functional interface
for downstream biological assays. In the generalized TCG chip
design, each RC is positioned downstream of the third hierar-
chical Row and is engineered to hold a fixed volume of 400
nanoliters (nL), providing discrete compartments for endpoint
analysis of the generated concentration gradient. To minimize
fluidic crosstalk between adjacent RCs and ensure independent
readouts, we conducted a dedicated 2-h experimental study to
evaluate spatial diffusion across chamber boundaries (Figure
S2). This analysis quantitatively assessed the influence of inter-
chamber spacing and connecting channel geometry on analyte
diffusion. The results revealed that the change in recorded
concentration values plateaued after initial stabilization, indi-
cating negligible cross-contamination within the 2-h incubation
window. These findings validated the current length and width
of the channels linking the RCs to the terminal Manifold,
confirming that the geometric configuration provides sufficient
isolation between RCs under typical assay durations, including
the LB-based incubation conditions used in microbiological
testing.
3.5
TCG Chip Analysis

operations, including agent preloading, buffer triggering, flow
synchronization, flow splitting, passive mixing, incubation, and
endpoint readout, enabling fully autonomous gradient generation
(Figure 2A). The workflow begins with manual preloading of 5 µL
of each agent into the designated Agent Inlets. These agents fill
the corresponding Agent Chambers and are halted precisely at
an interfacial spacing of 0.1 mm, separated by a central buffer-
activated control channel. This smart interface, located between
the Buffer Inlet and Agent Chambers, enables reliable flow initi-
ation and coordination without the need for external actuation.

from their chambers simultaneously, initiating synchronized flow
into the downstream manifold network. Accurate synchroniza-

agents are then the flow became split via a bifurcated channel
network that divides them into defined ratios before entering the
MCs. Within each MC, the divided fluid volumes are merged
and blended through convective-diffusive interactions governed
by the defined geometry. This merging-splitting-mixing cycle is

3.6
Two-Phase Capillary Fluid Flow Analysis
Reliable two-phase flow in capillary-driven microfluidic systems
requires synchronized fluid entry, uniform channel filling, and
the elimination of air entrapment at merging junctions. The
TCG chip architecture was optimized across four configurations
to ensure precise fluid entry into the MCs, enabling consistent
initial interaction between the agents and buffer. Preloaded

===PAGE 9===
agents are initially held at the interface between the Agent
Chambers and the buffer channel, and upon buffer loading,
capillary forces propel them forward, converging at the merging
zones within the manifold network. To achieve robust and
reproducible concentration gradients, the MC–Manifold archi-
tecture was designed to support balanced fluid distribution via
repeated splitting and recombination across three hierarchical
Rows. However, such merging points inherently present a risk
of bubble formation where opposing flows meet. To prevent
this, the MCs were constructed using a symmetrical layout,
ensuring that the two fluid fronts arrive simultaneously at the
junctions. This synchronization depends on matching hydraulic
resistance and capillary pressure at the merging points (Equations
S1 and S2), both of which are governed by channel geometry.
For agents of similar viscosity and surface tension, identical
geometric parameters are critical to maintain symmetrical flow
behavior throughout the network. In the standard TCG chip
design, MCs have a constant height of 100 µm and length
of 8 mm, with varying widths for configurations -a-, -b-, and
-c- (300, 500, and 700 µm, respectively). Configuration -d-
employs a 500 µm width with an additional pitch segment to
introduce curvature and extended length. These configurations
were fabricated to study the effects of geometric tuning on
fluid performance and bubble prevention. The effectiveness of
these designs was validated during the filling stage, as visualized
in Figure 2A for configuration -d-. The fluid fronts advanced
through all three hierarchical Rows of MCs without bubble
formation, confirming successful synchronization and reliable
operation. Additional validations for configurations -a-, -b-, and
-c- demonstrating similar bubble-free behaviour are provided in
Figures S3–S5.
3.7
Channels Geometry and Capillary Filling
Time
The filling times for the three hierarchical Rows of MCs across
the four TCG chip configurations are shown in Figure 2B. In
capillary-driven systems, flow behaviour is governed by two com-
peting parameters: hydraulic resistance and capillary pressure,
both of which are inversely related to channel width and height
Equations (S1) and (S2); S2.5. S2.5. Microchannel hydraulic resis-
tance and capillary pressure. Narrower channels exhibit higher
hydraulic resistance, while simultaneously generating higher
capillary pressure; however, the net effect typically results in
reduced flow rates due to the nonlinear dependence of resistance
on channel dimensions (Figure S6).
Following the hydraulic–electric circuit analogy, the volumet-
ric flow rate is proportional to the capillary pressure and
inversely proportional to the hydraulic resistance per unit length.
Accordingly, narrower MCs exhibit slower filling compared to
wider channels in configurations -b- and -c- (500 and 700 µm,
respectively), as observed in Figure 2B. In addition to slower
filling, configuration -a- also displayed greater variability in filling
times. This inconsistency may stem from fabrication-induced
wall roughness caused by laser micromachining, which has a
proportionally larger impact on narrow channels (Figure S8). In
microscale geometries, surface roughness disrupts contact line
stability and introduces local variations in capillary pressure,
compromising reproducibility.
To further investigate the effect of geometry on filling dynamics
and mitigate fabrication sensitivity, we designed configuration
-d- by incorporating a longer MC with an additional pitch
segment Figure 1C and Figure 2B). This modification introduced
expansion–reduction zones that increased the effective hydraulic
resistance, leading to controlled flow deceleration and longer
filling times. Importantly, this approach enabled tuning of flow
rates through structural design alone without altering channel
height or material surface properties.
3.8
Mixing Process Analysis
The MCs form the fundamental components of the TCG chip,
facilitating effective sample mixing and precise gradient gener-
ation. These components are accurately designed to manipulate
fluid flow through folding, stretching, and diffusive mixing,
providing controlled distribution to downstream elements. The
MCs, positioned in each hierarchical Row of the TCG chip,
cumulatively contribute to the total concentration gradient at
the outflow of each MCs Row via the Manifolds. To guarantee
the precise and uniform generation of stable concentration
gradients, which is essential for accurate downstream applica-
tions, it is imperative to thoroughly study the mixing process
in each TCG chip configuration. This requires evaluating the
mixing performance of individual MCs by analyzing fluid flow
at their Top cross-section (T-cs) and Bottom cross-section (B-
cs) zones (Figure 1D) [45, 46]. Figure 2C illustrates images of
a representative middle MCs from the first Row of each TCG
chip configurations -a-, -b-, -c-, and -d-, showing their geometric
variations and the progression of mixing within a single MC.

Concentration (NC) of each agent (NC-Ag1 and NC-Ag2) is
determined by comparing the concentration at each required
point with the Agent chamber concentration, which serves as
the maximum reference (see S3). This normalization ensures a
standardized assessment of mixing performance across the TCG
chip. To quantitatively assess the mixing performance within
each MC, the Mixing Index (MI) [21, 47–51] is defined as:

Figure 2E,a presents the NC profiles of NC-Ag1 measured at

the MC, the B-cs profile becomes significantly more uniform,

flow rate in Row 1. Higher flow rates amplify convective–diffusive
interactions, accelerating mixing efficiency. However, as the
sample fills more of the microchannel network, the hydraulic
resistance increases proportionally with length (as described in
Equation S1), gradually reducing the flow rate and thus the
mixing momentum in downstream rows.

gradient, the scope for further mixing diminishes, highlighting
the nonlinear relationship between NC gradient strength and
incremental mixing efficiency across hierarchical rows.
In Row 3, the NC profiles at the T-cs and B-cs cross-sections
of the second and third MCs are evaluated to assess final-stage
mixing behavior (Figure 2E,c). Owing to the chip’s geometric

These results collectively confirm that the final Row maintains
strong mixing performance despite the declining concentration
gradients, reinforcing the reliability of the hierarchical TCG chip
design.

3.10
Machine Learning Model Development
In previous sections, the optimization of the TCG chip was
achieved through experimental configurations -a-, -b-, -c-, and -
d-. Additionally, CFD simulations were conducted to investigate
the capillary fluid flow and concentration gradient within the
TCG chips. The CFD simulation results were validated across
multiple stages, including preloading and filling of hierarchi-
cal rows as well as the RCs. These findings are displayed
through volume fraction and normalized concentration surfaces,
alongside comparative experimental images for various TCG
chip configurations. The close alignment of CFD simulations
with experimental results provides a cost-effective method for
further analysis, avoiding the high costs, labour intensity, and
extensive laboratory equipment requirements of experimental
designs. On the other hand, running CFD simulations for
each iteration during the optimization process of TCG chips
is computationally and resource-intensive. As an example, a
single CFD simulation run on a laptop with an 11th Gen Intel
Core i7-1185G7 processor, 32 GB RAM, and a base speed of
3.00 GHz can take approximately one to two weeks to be
completed. This lengthy runtime is further exacerbated when
evaluating multiple design iterations, in optimization workflows
that may require hundreds of iterations. While high-performance
computing (HPC) resources can be used to parallelize CFD
simulations and reduce overall runtime, the cost and accessibility
of such resources pose additional challenges. These limitations
highlight the necessity for an alternative approach that can
provide fast and accurate predictions of the TCG chip per-
formance without depending exclusively on resource-intensive
CFD simulations. To overcome these limitations, we developed
a neural network (NN) model, shown in Figure 3A, capable of
predicting concentration gradient in RCs based on the TCG chip
configuration.
To systematically explore the TCG chip design space, we devel-
oped an automated geometry-generation tool in Python for the
CFD simulations (Figure 3A,a). Details of the tool are provided in
S5. Using the COMSOL LiveLink interface, Python executed CFD
simulations based on the Phase Field and Transport of Diluted
Species modules (Figure 3A,b), and returned NC profiles along
the predefined cut lines across RCs (Figure 2F,a and Figure 3A,c).
A dataset comprising 400 distinct TCG chip geometries was
generated and compiled into a CSV file (Figure 3A,d). The result-
ing dataset provided the necessary input-output relationships
for data-driven predictive modelling, forming the foundation
for the NN model’s development. Then, the NN model was
constructed using TensorFlow and Keras, with the architecture
optimized through hyperparameter tuning using Keras Tuner.
The input features included the pitch number in the first
three Rows (P1, P2, P3) and the MCs width (W), as shown in
Figure 3A,e.
The output of the NN model consisted of two neurons represent-
ing the mean NC in the second and third RCs. We employed a
Hyperband tuner to efficiently search the hyperparameter space,
exploring various network depths (1-5 hidden layers), neuron
counts (8–68 per layer), dropout rates (0–0.5), and learning rates
(10−4 to 10−2). Through this search strategy, we identified the
optimal TCG chip configuration for the performance-prediction
task (Figure 3A,f). The dataset of 400 CFD simulations was
split into training (80%) and testing (20%) sets. The NN model
developed for predicting the concentration gradient in the TCG
chip demonstrated excellent performance. After training, the NN
model achieved a remarkably low loss of 1.9283e−4 (Figure 3B,a),
and a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.0099 (Figure 3B,b), on
the training set. The validation set showed similarly impressive
results, with a loss of 2.5094e−4 and an MAE of 0.0129, indicating
good generalization without overfitting. When evaluated on the
unseen test set, the model maintained its high performance,
achieving a MAE of 0.0104. This low MAE suggests that, on
average, the model’s predictions deviate from the actual NC
values by only about 1.04%, demonstrating its high accuracy in
predicting NC gradient across various chip designs. The model’s
predictive power is further evidenced by its R-squared value of
0.9392 on the test set, showcasing its strong capability to capture
the complex relationships between the TCG chip geometry and
resulting concentration gradients, as the performance indicators
are shown in Figure 3B,c. These results validate the effectiveness
of the NN approach in modelling the behaviour of TCG chips,
and the trained NN model is saved to be implemented in the
optimization process.
3.11
Optimization Framework for TCG Chip
Design Using CMDA
The design of the TCG chip presents a multi-objective opti-
mization challenge: achieving precise concentration gradients
in the RCs while adhering to manufacturability constraints and
practical design preferences. To address this, we implemented
the CMDA framework that integrates a trained NN for rapid
concentration prediction with a quasi-Newton optimization strat-
egy for design refinement. Specifically, we employed the L-BFGS
algorithm, a robust and scalable method for high-dimensional
optimization. This approach enables efficient navigation of the
TCG design space to identify configurations that yield desired out-
put gradients while remaining fabrication-feasible. The objective
function guiding the optimization incorporates multiple design
goals. Chief among these is the minimization of the error between
the predicted and target NCs in the RCs, denoted as C1 through

In addition to optimizing for concentration accuracy, secondary
objectives in the CMDA framework are designed to enhance
the practicality and robustness of the TCG chip. These include
minimizing the overall chip footprint and fluid path length,
achieved by penalizing the total number of mixing pitches
(P1 + P2 + P3), while simultaneously encouraging the use of
wider MCs to reduce fabrication variability and mitigate clogging
risks. Wider MCs are generally more tolerant to imperfections
introduced during laser cutting or bonding and support more
reliable capillary flow. The design space spans a discrete set of
pitch values (P ∈[0, 9]) and a continuous range of MC widths
(W ∈[300, 900 µm]). These bounds reflect manufacturability
constraints and ensure compatibility with capillary-driven flow
regimes, preserving device reliability and reproducibility. This
multi-objective optimization framework emphasizes the trade-off
between functional performance, structural reliability, and ease
of fabrication. By integrating these constraints directly into the
objective function, the algorithm promotes chip designs that are
not only functionally optimized but also robust and scalable for
real-world applications.

RCs were coated with a tetrazolium chloride (TTC) layer, a

progressive increase in bacterial concentration.
The conventional MIC assay is typically conducted using either
96-well plates or multiple test tubes for serial dilutions. In this
method, a series of dilutions of the test antimicrobial agent are
prepared in a suitable broth (Figure 5D,a). Each tube is inoculated
with a standardized concentration of the microorganism and
incubated under appropriate conditions. To incorporate different
microorganism concentrations, the assay must be extended,
requiring a separate row where the same serial dilution of the
drug is combined with the new bacterial concentration. This
process effectively doubles the time required for sampling and
pipetting. For example, Figure 5D,b shows the results of an
MIC assay testing the antibacterial effect of Gentamicin (0–
10 µg mL−1, in 1 µg mL−1 intervals) against E. coli at 106, 107,
and 108 CFU mL−1. In such a setup, acquiring and inspecting
one specific E. coli and gentamicin concentration requires the
operator to prepare an entire set of tubes. However, the auto-
mixing capability of the TCG chips simplifies this process by
enabling the acquisition of specific concentrations in a single
step. Given the initial loading conditions at Agent inlets 1 and
2, defined output compositions can be generated across the RCs,

MIC-oriented susceptibility screening demonstration rather than
a direct replacement for standardized broth microdilution meth-

across RCs while varying only the antibiotic concentration to
align with standard MIC protocols.
While the current TCG-CMDA chip has been validated for
testing a single antibiotic, this represents a strategically important
first-line antibiotic, such as gentamicin or ampicillin, that is
commonly used as empirical treatment for life-threatening infec-
tions. In particular, patients with sepsis, urinary tract infections
(UTIs), or neonatal infections benefit most from rapid first-line
antibiotic susceptibility testing. In sepsis cases, every hour of
delayed appropriate antibiotic therapy increases mortality by
7.6%, and up to 56% of sepsis patients initially receive inappro-
priate antibiotics due to lack of timely diagnostics. Critically
ill patients in emergency departments, long-term care facilities,
and low-resource rural settings often receive empiric treatment
without guidance. By enabling reliable testing with a colorimetric
readout and a simple smartphone camera, without the need for
pumps or external equipment, our chip offers a true POC solution.
This can be transformative in frontline clinics, nursing homes,
military deployments, and emergency settings where centralized
laboratory testing is unavailable or delayed. Studies suggest that
up to 60%–70% of empirical antibiotic use in such settings could
be improved with access to rapid AST, underlining the urgent
clinical value of our single-antibiotic POC assay.
Building on this foundation, we aim to expand the TCG-CMDA
platform to enable multiplex testing for multiple antibiotics or
therapeutic agents within a single chip. Importantly, we have
already developed an AI-driven platform capable of generating
and optimizing microfluidic geometries tailored to desired con-
centration outputs. Leveraging this capability, the next step is to
apply AI-guided design automation to create combinatorial assay
layouts featuring parallel and/or serial reaction chambers. This
will facilitate seamless integration with standard commercial
AST panels and allow for efficient profiling of multidrug resis-
tance. The programmable architecture, combined with capillary
operation and camera-based colorimetric readout, positions our
platform as a scalable and low-cost diagnostic solution for decen-
tralized healthcare, ultimately advancing toward comprehensive,
high-throughput AST directly at the point of care.
4
Conclusion
This study introduces the Tree-Shaped Concentration Gradient
Generator enabled by Capillary Microfluidic Design Automation
(TCG-CMDA) which is a transformative platform that integrates
capillary-driven fluidics, CFD, and neural network-guided opti-
mization to autonomously generate programmable concentration
gradients. Demonstrated in the context of MIC testing for E. coli
and gentamicin, the TCG chip achieved high precision (<5% devi-
ation) and reproducibility across replicates. Its passive, pump-free
operation supports fully automated workflows without external
instrumentation, requiring only a camera for readout, and is com-
patible with low-cost, scalable manufacturing techniques such
as laser cutting. The CMDA pipeline accelerates design iteration
through a quasi-Newton optimization loop embedded within a
machine learning surrogate model, establishing a reproducible,
data-driven path for intelligent lab-on-chip design.
Beyond its technical innovations, the TCG-CMDA chip holds
immediate clinical value as a truly POC diagnostic tool. Its ability
to rapidly assess susceptibility to a single first-line antibiotic can
be life-saving in critical care situations such as sepsis, where
each hour of delay in effective antibiotic administration increases
mortality risk by 7.6%. The chip portability and visual, camera-
based detection make it ideal for deployment in low-resource
settings, emergency departments, long-term care homes, and
field hospitals. Looking forward, we plan to expand the TCG-
CMDA platform for multiplexed AST applications by leveraging
our existing AI design framework to configure chips for multi-
antibiotic screening and a variety of sample viscosities. The
modular design allows parallelization of reaction chambers to test
multiple drugs in a single run, paving the way for compatibility
with clinical AST panels and resistance profiling protocols.
The TCG-CMDA platform demonstrates how predictive mod-
eling, automated design, and capillary microfluidics can be
integrated into a reproducible workflow for programmable gradi-
ent generation. In this study, this capability was validated for MIC
testing using E. coli and gentamicin. As application-specific vali-
dations beyond this scope were not performed, broader uses of the
framework should be considered prospective extensions rather
than experimentally demonstrated outcomes. Nevertheless, the
architecture is readily adaptable to expanded chip layouts, multi-
agent gradient generation, and integration with sensing modules
in future developments.
